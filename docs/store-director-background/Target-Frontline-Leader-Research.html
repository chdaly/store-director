Target · Frontline Leader Research

Target Persona — Discovery + Research

Discovery synthesis, use cases to land, and the open questions that gate them.

Sources: Target Store Director future-state persona materials; Store Director call notes; Supply Chain + Site Director discovery call (Jennifer Rider, Jennifer Hunter, Nicholas Coulter, Nathan Campbell, Benjamin Kremer). Compiled by Karin Smith.

Two audiences, one pattern

Both groups are senior leaders whose leadership work is being displaced by assembly work. What differs is what they are assembling.

Store Director

Give Store Directors their leadership job back — time out of task mode, spent coaching their teams and observing their stores.

The bottleneck is comms and orchestration. They drown in messages, reading, and coordinating other people’s initiatives.

Signature proof point: ~12 projects, ~50 dates, different owners across multiple pyramids, separate contacts for each.

What gets displaced: store observations — the feedback loop that finds improvements.

Supply Chain + Site Director

Site Directors spend their mornings assembling the health of the building by hand. Give them that picture on arrival, and give the time back to talent and culture.

The bottleneck is data. They drown in reports, dashboards, and reconciliation.

Signature proof point: 90+ minutes every morning pulling data, reviewing it, and prioritizing.

What gets displaced: talent and culture — upskilling and development.

Shared ground

UKG scheduling pain, heavy Excel use, mornings that start in email, and leadership time crowded out by task mode. Both audiences describe the same displacement with different mechanics — which means one narrative can carry both, provided the proof points stay specific to each.

How to make it land

Do not lead with capabilities. Capture one or two vivid moments — an SD connecting dots or leading down a level, a Site Director cross-checking two systems that disagree — and in the follow-up, play that exact moment back, solved.

Nothing sells meaningful change like their own building’s example. The 90-minute figure and the 12-projects/50-dates figure are the two strongest numbers available; both are theirs, and both convert directly into leadership time.

Give Store Directors their leadership job back — time out of task mode, spent coaching their teams and observing their stores.

Time saved is the mechanism. Coaching capacity is the outcome. Always land on the outcome.

What they own

  • Accountability for total store results, with recognition and reward aligned to store performance
  • Bringing the Target guest experience to life
  • Store culture and recognition
  • Developing and sustaining a strong leadership pipeline
  • Setting clear expectations and priorities
  • Store brand image and how the team shows up as brand ambassadors
  • Community engagement and connection

What we heard

1The job has become a translation job

This is the headline insight. Corporate produces content and initiatives in one shape — workstream-specific, pyramid-specific, calendar-specific. The SD manually converts it into another: store-specific, day-specific, person-specific.

  • Reading arrives in the wrong shape. Workstream-specific, not store-specific, so SDs deconstruct and customize it.
  • The work is duplicated across the fleet. Every SD independently performs the same translation.
  • It happens off the clock. Increasingly it starts at home, before the shift. The pre-work is the workaround.

2Orchestration without coordination

Multiple pyramids push initiatives to stores independently. The SD becomes the integration layer — reconciling dates, trainings and reading, and chasing confirmation from in-store ICs. Twelve projects and fifty dates is the shape of it, with a different owner and contact behind each.

3Comms require triage and confirmation

MyDayComms is not just reading. It is triage plus confirming completion — a closed-loop obligation stacked on the information load, compounding across every channel.

4Analysis pulls them off the floor

Heavy Excel users. The data exists, but reaching it costs floor presence. Every hour in a spreadsheet is an hour not leading.

5Scheduling

Effective scheduling is a significant pain point. UKG implementation is in progress.

6Store observations are what suffer

Seeing where improvements can be made is the first thing cut. This is the sharpest casualty: not just lost time, but the feedback loop that finds improvements going dark. Problems stay invisible until they surface in the metrics.

7Where they want to spend time

Coaching and leadership development is where SDs feel most like leaders. They want out of task mode — and want to help their teams get out of task mode too.

Compounding effect: an SD trapped in execution cannot coach their leaders out of execution either. The persona’s “leading down a level” problem replicates one layer further down. Fixing it at the SD level unlocks the whole leadership pipeline.

Persona pain points, for reference

The five stated pain points from the Target materials, which the call notes confirm and sharpen.

  • Workload & workforce volatility — coverage gaps pull SDs into constant rebalancing instead of building a stable, developed team.
  • Unclear prioritization — everything feels high-priority, with no tradeoff guidance when labor is constrained.
  • Lack of empowerment to lead — SDs default to execution and “lead down a level.”
  • Disconnected tools & communication channels — no single view of what matters.
  • Constantly shifting focus — shifting field-leader priorities create frequent firefighting.

Copilot adoption — the strongest evidence

Already using it for

  • Documenting best practices
  • Leadership development
  • Auto-adding dates and action items to the calendar
  • Analyzing guest feedback
  • Building a team map with responsibilities

Why it matters: not a cold audience. They self-selected the highest-value use cases unprompted, and nearly every one maps to the persona’s ownership list. Given a tool that removes friction, SDs spend the time on leadership.

What they want next

  • More detailed review of emails
  • Better at adding multiple dates to the calendar
  • Using it for comms and tracking
  • Prompting help to get the right answers
  • Report creation — one SD built their own dashboard because the existing one was not useful

Why it matters: the ask list is almost a one-to-one match with the pain points. When a user routes around official tooling to build their own dashboard, that is not a feature request — it is evidence the current system does not serve them.

Correlation to test

Stores with low guest metrics also appear to carry operational issues. If observation is what suffers most, that may be the connective tissue — less observation means fewer improvements caught, showing up in both operations and guest scores. Worth testing, not asserting. If it holds, it turns “give SDs time back” into a measurable performance argument in Target’s own language.

Site Directors spend their mornings assembling the health of the building by hand. Give them that picture on arrival, and give the time back to talent and culture.

The mechanism is data assembly — pulling, cross-checking, reconciling. The outcome is upskilling and talent development, which is where they say they want to spend their time.

What they are accountable for

  • Safety — the highest priority, stated explicitly and unambiguously
  • Financials
  • Throughput
  • Productivity and performance
  • Talent, culture, and development
How the day starts

Recaps arrive by email. They review reports to establish the current “health of the building.” Calendars are updated in the morning as they determine what to prioritize and who to connect with. The plan of day is constructed, not delivered — 90 minutes or more, every morning.

What we heard

1Email triage: inform versus action

Sorting informational email from email that requires action is a challenge, and it is the gate on building the plan of day. Prioritizing action out of the inbox came up as a distinct theme, separate from the volume problem.

2“Health of the building” is assembled by hand

The morning report review exists to answer one question: what is the state of this site right now? Today that answer has to be built rather than read, and three things make it expensive:

  • Multiple sources. Origami, Greenfield, Workday and Apollo each hold part of the picture.
  • Conflicting data quality. Sources disagree, so cross-checking must happen before the data can be trusted.
  • Reconciliation on top of retrieval. The cross-check is work layered on the pull — together, the clearest driver of the 90-minute morning.

3Visibility gaps: single-site, single-moment

Dashboards provide a single-site view with little visibility into multi-site or enterprise views. Trending over time is also difficult to see — “good catches” was the example given. The picture they can assemble is not only manual, it is narrow: one building, right now.

4Data is hard to reach, hard to shape, hard to go deep in

Sources can be large or not specific enough, and data is layered and hard to find — which makes checking in and catching blindspots harder than it should be. Views are difficult to customize, and they want more advanced analytics to dig deeper rather than stopping where the dashboard stops.

5Data entry could be streamlined

Not all types are known, but safety data entry specifically was called out as a candidate for streamlining.

6Report once, present many

They take action on data and then present it back in different views to different audiences — PowerPoint and Excel. Reporting splits into views for “outside the building” and “inside the building.” Each audience view is rebuilt by hand today.

7Weekly staffing optimization

A named challenge with a defined process. To build the weekly plan they look at:

  • Site demand
  • Labor availability (UKG — new tool)
  • Who is volunteering
  • Overtime impact
  • Special projects and training that may consume resources
  • A lookback on performance, attendance and volunteers

8Where they want to spend time

Talent and culture — upskilling, content, and talent development. The same pattern as Store Directors: the leadership work is what gets displaced by the assembly work.

What they asked about

Raised directly by participants, unprompted.

  • Scheduled prompts — a Sunday run to make the start of the week easier
  • Notebooks — explicit interest in understanding how they could help
  • Prompts for consistent reporting and views — asked as an open question
  • Connecting UKG — flagged as a new tool, with connection raised as a possibility
  • More advanced analytics — the ability to dig deeper into the data
  • End-of-shift hand-off and next best action — a potential low-hanging use case, depending on data availability

Store visit — what to observe

Signals

  • The morning routine — what they read before arriving, and what they check first once in the building.
  • Disconnected tools — inventory every distinct device, app and screen the SD and team leads touch. Literally count them. Watch for reconciling two screens by hand or re-keying.
  • Prioritization — how the day’s priorities are communicated; whether a single visible “what matters most today” exists.
  • Workload volatility — how coverage is displayed and how often it is re-jockeyed; whether the SD is covering a task rather than leading.
  • Interruptions — count redirections during the walk, and what triggers each.
  • Empowerment — which decisions are made on the spot versus escalated.

By pillar

Guest — in-stock and on-shelf availability, checkout and guest services wait times, live guest recovery, friction points.

Team — huddles, visible recognition, onboarding of new or seasonal members, shift-to-shift handoffs.

Operations — backroom and fulfillment flow, task tracking, exception handling, paper versus digital, duplicate entry.

Financials — which dashboards are actually used, data freshness, one view or many.

Ownership cross-check — culture and recognition (visible or ad hoc?), leadership pipeline (developing TLs or filling gaps?), brand image, community engagement.

Site visit — what to observe

The daily rhythm

  • The morning routine — which systems open first, how many logins, where cross-checking happens, and how the plan of day gets written down.
  • Data assembly — count the sources touched to answer one question. Watch for reconciling two screens by hand, re-keying, or rebuilding the same view.
  • Dashboards in use — which are actually opened, how current the data is, and what gets exported to Excel immediately after.
  • Shift hand-off — what is captured, how, and what is lost between shifts.

The work products

  • Data entry — where it happens, how long it takes, and whether the same fact is entered twice. Safety entry specifically.
  • Reporting — what gets rebuilt in PowerPoint or Excel, for whom, and how often.
  • Staffing build — if timing allows, observe the weekly plan being assembled and note each input source.
  • Safety — how safety data surfaces in the daily rhythm, and whether trending (good catches) is visible or has to be requested.

How to make it land

Do not lead with capabilities. Capture one or two vivid moments — an SD connecting dots or leading down a level, a Site Director cross-checking two systems that disagree — and in the follow-up, play that exact moment back, solved. Nothing sells meaningful change like their own building’s example.
